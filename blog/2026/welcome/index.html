From Chicago to Mumbai: A Year of Shifting Gears and Building Foundations

Created on January 10, 2026

2026   ·   reflection   goals   career   ·   personal

As I look back on 2025, the word that comes to mind is agency. It was the year I stopped waiting for growth to happen to me and started engineering it myself. From managing my first team of interns in Chicago to relocating across the world to Mumbai, the last twelve months have been a masterclass in navigating ambiguity and technical ownership.

The Chicago Chapter: High Output, Low Depth

The first half of 2025 at IMC was a study in efficiency versus evolution. Working across the valuation stack, I found myself in a comfortable rhythm: I understood the underlying mathematical models and could translate them into clean, functional features with speed.

On paper, I was performing at a high level, but internally, I felt a plateau. I realized that much of my work consisted of BAU (Business As Usual) tasks—the kind of logical translation that, while necessary, lacked the architectural depth I craved. It was a sobering realization: these were precisely the types of tasks I expect AI to automate in the near future. I was mastering a skill set that was becoming a commodity, and I knew I needed to find a more complex “frontier” to stay ahead.

A Pivot to Leadership: Mentoring Interns

While my technical growth felt like it was hitting a ceiling, a new avenue for development opened up: management. I stepped into a lead role for two interns, a full-circle moment given that I had started my own journey in that very same program. This transition from “individual contributor” to “technical guide” provided the spark I was missing.

Seeing my interns evolve from their first day to a successful project conclusion was incredibly rewarding, but more importantly, it was my first real calibration of leadership. I learned that guiding a team toward a technical goal requires a completely different “compiler”—one built on communication, project scoping, and the ability to unblock others rather than just writing the code myself.

Moving to Mumbai

Mid-year, I made a big move—literally. I asked for the opportunity to work out of our Mumbai office, and I’m grateful the company backed me on it.

The environment here is a stark contrast to the US. The office is smaller and more “chaotic” in the best way possible. Without a massive density of local experts to lean on, I’ve had to lean on myself. This shift has forced two major changes in my workflow:

  1. Project Management as a Survival Skill: My first project here spanned several teams. Navigating a smaller office meant building rapport from scratch and learning to structure communication more rigorously to keep things moving.
  2. Active vs. Passive Learning: In Chicago, I realized that technical growth doesn’t just “happen” through osmosis. In Mumbai, I’ve used my free time to get back into “student mode.” But this time, it’s different.

Moving from “Learning” to “Building”

The biggest shift in the latter half of 2025 was breaking a lifelong habit of passive learning. Historically, my approach to growth was rooted in high-volume consumption. I used to pride myself on the “grind”—at one point during my time at UC Berkeley, I took ten math courses in a single semester. I was very good at grinding through homework assignments and taking exams, but I was collecting knowledge without ever “compiling” it. There is a big difference between taking a course and engaging deeply with a subject.

The biggest shift in the latter half of 2025 was moving away from passive consumption. When I started the CS231n course and Karpathy’s makemore, I didn’t just watch the videos. I built the models and testing frameworks from scratch.

I’ve realized that I learn best when I’m breaking things. To date, I’ve built three project prototypes in the last few months alone. Using AI as a force multiplier rather than a crutch has allowed me to move faster, but the core architectural decisions remain mine.

Looking Ahead: My 2026 North Stars

If 2025 was about realizing where the gaps were, 2026 is about closing them with precision. I’m focusing on three pillars:

1. The “Deep Dive” Cadence

I am committing to learning one new technical topic every month. Whether it’s a technical book or a specialized course, I’m going deep. To ensure the knowledge sticks, I’ll be writing a technical blog post for each one to summarize the core mechanics.

2. From Prototype to Market

Building in a vacuum is great for learning, but building for users is where the real challenge lies. In 2026, I want to take at least one of my project ideas and bring it to market. Learning the “product” side—user feedback, deployment, and scaling—is the next logical step in my evolution as an engineer.

3. Mastering the Art of Communication

Technical skills are the floor; communication is the ceiling. I have two specific goals here:

  • Socially: As a natural introvert, I’m pushing myself out of my comfort zone. I’m making it a point to join local meetup groups and hackathons in Mumbai to build my social “muscle memory” and mingle with a broader technical community.
  • Professionally: My primary goal is to communicate complex logic and system designs with the same clarity and precision with which I write code. Often, there is a “lossy” translation when explaining valuation stacks or architectural trade-offs to non-technical stakeholders. I want my verbal explanations to have the same “readability” as a well-documented repository. To help bridge this gap, I’m starting a course from Vinh Giang to learn how to structure my thoughts. Learning how to communicate effectively is a lifelong journey and these are the first steps towards improving actively.

2025 was the year of the move. 2026 will be the year of the build. Let’s get to work.

Here’s to a year of learning, building, and growing! 🚀
